The problem

I read in three languages and meet words I do not know in all of them. By the time I have finished the paragraph I have lost the word, and if I have not lost the word I have lost the sentence, which is the part that teaches you how it is used.

The apps for this exist. They want me to type the word in, then type the meaning in, then type an example in. That is three steps between seeing a word and keeping it, and I was not doing them.

What I built

Two halves: catching and studying. A bookmarklet sits on the favourites bar. Select a word on any page, click it, and the word is saved with the sentence around it, the page title and the link. Nothing else to fill in.

The meaning arrives on its own. Wiktionary for the definitions and an example, Simple Wiktionary for a plainer version of the same thing, with the full dictionary one button away. Wikipedia as a last resort, which is how a word like evals gets an answer at all.

Three languages, three separate spaces. English, Spanish and German do not share a pile. Each keeps its own words, its own decks and its own sitting, and switching between them is one click.

Turkish on the back, when there is one. Wiktionary keeps translation tables written by people, one per sense, so prudent comes back as ihtiyatlı for the careful sense and tutumlu for the thrifty one. Four sources are asked in all. Where none of them knows, it says so.

Three answers, not two. Not yet, Almost, Knew it. Not yet leaves the card due today, Almost pushes it two days out, Knew it sixteen. Flat gaps rather than a doubling ladder, because I study twice a day and a ladder sent everything to next month by Thursday.

Real voices. English, Spanish and German recordings come from Wikimedia Commons, made by people. German brings its phonetic spelling with it. Where no recording exists the browser says the word in that language’s own voice, which is worse and still better than guessing.

German nouns carry their article. The card says das Haus, not Haus. A German noun without its gender is a noun learned wrong, and the gender is not guessable from the word.

It works on the phone, and offline. Added to the home screen it opens like an app, with no browser chrome and no address bar. Sign in with Google and the words follow you between the laptop and the phone; stay signed out and they never leave the browser you saved them in.

What I learned

A dictionary answers the word you asked about, not the word you met. Save vetted and Wiktionary says “past tense of vet”, so the card follows the pointer to vet and leads with its first meaning, which is a veterinarian. The fix is to notice that only verbs have a past tense, and put the verb senses first. Both are still there. Only the order changed.

Machine translation is worse than no translation. I tried it for the Turkish before the translation tables. It returned çekirdek for quorum and ubikuitöz for ubiquitous. A wrong answer in a vocabulary tool is worse than a blank, because you will learn the wrong answer.

A full stop is not a sentence boundary. The bookmarklet grabs the sentence around the word, and the first version cut “$1.25 trillion” in half and started the sentence at “25 trillion”. A boundary is a stop, followed by a space, not preceded by a digit or a lone capital letter.

A deck that is only a label is not a deck. Decks started as a word written on each card, so the list was assembled by reading every card. Move the last word out of a deck and the deck did not become empty, it stopped existing. You also could not make one in advance and fill it later, because there was nothing to write the name on.

Storage you did not write is hostile. One malformed field in the browser’s own storage took the whole page down to a blank screen. Everything read back out now goes through one sanitiser, which also catches anything arriving from an import file.

Stack

Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, no framework and no build step. Cloudflare Workers and D1 for the sign-in and the sync. Wiktionary, Simple Wiktionary, Wikipedia, Wikidata and Wikimedia Commons for everything on the card. Written with Claude Code.
