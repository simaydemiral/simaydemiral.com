Ledger

Drop in a bank statement and see where the money actually went — without the statement ever leaving your computer.

Every budgeting app I looked at wanted my bank login. That is the part I was not willing to give, and it is also the part that is hardest to justify: a tool that reads a CSV does not need a password to my account, and it does not need a server either.

Try it with your own statement → Your file stays in your browser

The problem

I could see every transaction and still not answer a simple question: what do I actually spend on restaurants in a month? The bank shows a list. A list is not an answer.

The honest obstacle was not effort, it was trust. Linking a bank account to a third party to find out how much I spend on groceries is a bad trade.

What I built

A single page that does everything locally. You drop a CSV on it; the browser reads the file, works out which columns are which, cleans up the merchant names, sorts transactions into categories and draws the charts. There is no backend, no account, and no upload.

It makes no network requests at all — no fonts, no analytics, no CDN. You can open the network tab while you use it and watch it stay empty. That is checkable, which is the only kind of privacy claim worth making.

What I learned along the way

Reading the CSV was the easy part. Every bank writes a different file: one had four summary lines and a blank row before the real header, a running balance column sitting next to the amount, and a first row whose amount was empty. Picking the wrong column silently inverts the whole dashboard, so the parser identifies columns by sampling the values rather than trusting the headings, and it refuses to import anything it is unsure about.

The bug that mattered most was quieter. I was counting money moved between my own accounts as spending — paying my credit card from savings looked like $2,871 of purchases. Nothing in the totals looked obviously wrong, which is exactly why it survived. Internal transfers are now identified and excluded from every figure.

Categorising merchants turned out to be a naming problem. Banks write SQ *BLUE BOTTLE COFFE and AMZN Mktp US*2X4RT1, so the first job is stripping the processor prefixes, store numbers, phone numbers and city codes to get back to a name a human would recognise. My first version stripped too hard: UBER *PENDING lost its brand and became "Pendi", and FRONTIER AI — which is Frontier Airlines — was very nearly filed under software.

The decision that made the rest work is that a transaction never stores its own category. Both the merchant name and the category are recomputed every time the page loads, from the rules plus your corrections. So fixing one merchant fixes it across your whole history and every future import, and improving the parser reaches transactions imported months ago. Only three things are actually saved: the raw rows, your corrections, and your pending list.

Rent and tuition were the last problem. They are real spending but they dwarf everything else, and leaving them in meant the chart said "you spent a lot on rent" every single month. They are now reported separately, so the headline figure is the part you can do something about.
