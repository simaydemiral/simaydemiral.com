Ledger

Drop in a bank statement and see where the money actually went — without the statement ever leaving your computer.

My bank's app shows me every transaction and not much else. It cannot tell me what I spend on restaurants in a month, it will not let me fix a category it got wrong, and it has no idea when the same charge comes through twice. Ledger is the dashboard I wanted instead.

Try it with your own statement → Your file stays in your browser

The problem

I can already see my spending in banking apps, but what I cannot do is ask them anything like:

  • How much went to outside eating this month, and is that more than usual?
  • Which refunds am I still waiting on?
  • Did I pay that bill twice?
  • Where did the money go to a name I half remember?

The things I wanted were ordinary. Put a merchant in the right category myself and have it stay there. Search the history by name. Keep track of refunds. Compare this month's spending against past months. Get told when something is out of line. There are apps that do all of this, but that is a lot to hand over to find out how much I spend on groceries, which is why I ended up building it myself.

What I built

Everything runs in the browser. You drop a CSV — your bank statements — on the page. It reads the file, works out which columns are which, cleans up the merchant names, sorts transactions into categories and draws the charts. There is no backend and no account.

Every category is editable. The rules get some merchants wrong. Change one and it applies to that merchant everywhere — your whole history and every import after it, not just the row you clicked.

Refunds and pending charges are tracked too. You can tag a refund so it nets off properly, and log a pending charge before it clears; when the real transaction arrives, the two are matched. The whole history is searchable by merchant.

Subscriptions get their own tab. Anything billed on a steady cadence — same merchant, similar amount, three times or more — is collected there, with what you pay, when it is next due and what it has cost you so far.

Double charges get flagged. When the same merchant charges the same amount twice within a few days, the month flags it. There is a floor on the amount, so two coffees in one afternoon do not count.

It makes no network requests at all. No fonts, no analytics, no CDN. You can open the network tab while you use it and watch it stay empty.

What I learned along the way

Bank files are messy, so the parser can’t blindly trust the headers. One file had four summary lines, a blank row before the real header, and a running balance next to the amount. The parser now identifies columns based on the values and refuses to import anything it’s unsure about.

Good categorization starts with getting the merchant name right. Banks turn merchants into strings like AMZN Mktp US*2X4RT1, so the parser strips the noise to recover a recognizable merchant name. But it has to be careful not to strip too much — FRONTIER AI, for example, is actually Frontier Airlines.

The key design decision was to recompute categories instead of storing them on each transaction. Merchant names and categories are recalculated from the rules and my corrections every time the page loads, so fixing one merchant updates my entire history and future imports. Only the raw rows, corrections, and pending list are saved.

A dashboard is only useful if it shows meaningful insights you can act on. Separating fixed commitments like rent and tuition from day-to-day spending made the numbers much more meaningful. The goal isn’t to show me that I spent $2,150 on rent — I already know that. It’s to surface the spending patterns I can actually change.
