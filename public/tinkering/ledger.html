Ledger

Drop in a bank statement and see where the money actually went — without the statement ever leaving your computer.

My bank's app shows me every transaction and not much else. It cannot tell me what I spend on restaurants in a month, it will not let me fix a category it got wrong, and it has no idea when the same charge comes through twice. Ledger is the dashboard I wanted instead.

Try it with your own statement → Your file stays in your browser

The problem

I can already see my spending in banking apps, but what I cannot do is ask them anything like:

  • How much went to outside eating this month, and is that more than usual?
  • Which refunds am I still waiting on?
  • Did I pay that bill twice?
  • Where did the money go to a name I half remember?

The things I wanted were ordinary. Put a merchant in the right category myself and have it stay there. Search the history by name. Keep track of refunds. Compare this month's spending against past months. Get told when something is out of line. There are apps that do all of this, but that is a lot to hand over to find out how much I spend on groceries, which is why I ended up building it myself.

What I built

A single page that does everything locally. You drop a CSV (your bank statements) on it; the browser reads the file, works out which columns are which, cleans up the merchant names, sorts transactions into categories and draws the charts.

The rules get some merchants wrong, so every category is editable. Change one and it applies to that merchant everywhere — your whole history and every import after it, not just the row you clicked. You can tag refunds, log a pending charge before it clears and have it matched to the real transaction when it arrives, and search the history by merchant.

Two things it works out on its own. Anything billed on a steady cadence — same merchant, similar amount, three times or more — gets collected on a Subscriptions tab, with what you pay, when it is next due and what it has cost you so far. And when the same merchant charges the same amount twice within a few days, the month flags it, with a floor on the amount so two coffees in one afternoon do not count.

It makes no network requests at all — no fonts, no analytics, no CDN. You can open the network tab while you use it and watch it stay empty.

What I learned along the way

Every bank writes a different file. One of mine had four summary lines and a blank row before the real header, a running balance column sitting next to the amount, and a first row with no amount at all. Picking the wrong column silently inverts the whole dashboard, so the parser works out the columns by looking at the values rather than trusting the headings, and it refuses to import anything it is unsure about.

I load a credit card, a current account and savings, and those accounts pay each other constantly. The first version counted that as spending — paying the card from savings looked like $2,871 of purchases. Nothing in the totals looked wrong, which is why it survived so long. Transfers between your own accounts are now recognized and left out of every figure, so loading three files does not count the same dollar three times.

Categorizing is really a naming problem. Banks write AMZN Mktp US*2X4RT1, so the first job is stripping the processor prefixes, store numbers, phone numbers and city codes to get back to a name a person would recognize. Strip too hard and the brand goes with it: FRONTIER AI — which is Frontier Airlines — was very nearly filed under software.

The decision that made the rest work is that a transaction never stores its own category. Both the merchant name and the category are recomputed every time the page loads, from the rules plus your corrections. So fixing one merchant fixes it across your whole history and every future import, and improving the parser reaches transactions imported months ago. Only three things are actually saved: the raw rows, your corrections, and your pending list.

The last fix was the fixed commitments (e.g., rent, tuition). They are real spending, but they are so much larger than everything else that leaving them in made the chart say "you spent a lot on rent" every single month. They now get their own line, so the headline number is the part you can actually do something about.
