Crypto groups commit over $200 million to midterms

Crypto-tied organizations have contributed more than $200 million this cycle, outpacing online betting and data center groups in federal corporate political spending according to Public Citizen, with Fairshake and affiliated super PACs backing 33 House candidates and putting at least $1 million each behind six incumbents. The push follows the Clarity Act stalling in the Senate in September after Democrats argued it did too little to stop top officials profiting from crypto ventures, and comes as voters focus elsewhere — Rep. Steven Horsford, one of the Democrats receiving over $1 million, said the overwhelming issue right now is the affordability crisis.

Late-forming super PACs spend $637 million on midterms

Twenty-two super PACs that registered in the final 10 weeks before Election Day have each spent at least $1 million on congressional races, reporting roughly $637 million in independent expenditures by Oct. 29, according to a CNBC analysis of FEC filings. Large expenditures must be disclosed within 24 or 48 hours but donors are reported less often, and both parties are using the tactic; Northwestern law professor Michael Kang said the reason is almost entirely to avoid disclosure, even if temporarily, while the Campaign Legal Center's Saurav Ghosh said it is happening more because it works.

KEY   22 PACs, each $1M+ · about $637M by Oct. 29 · roughly one-sixth of all House and Senate independent spending this cycle · more than 22% of independent expenditures in CNBC's 2026 window, up from under 2% · Republican-linked PACs spent about twice Democratic-linked ones · 18 of 22 began spending within 30 days of launching · 2022: five groups, about $18M · 2018: three groups, about $12M · under $50M combined in 2020 and 2024 · Our Heritage Matters registered Oct. 15, spent about $1.2M across 10 races by Oct. 29, donors due Dec. 3

Women in Turkish prisons up nearly twelvefold since 2005

The number of women held in Turkish prisons has risen roughly 11.9 times over 21 years, from 1,802 in 2005 to 21,445 in 2026, according to World Prison Brief data cited in an interview with CİSST representative Özge Akyüz. Akyüz said the system is organized around men's needs and raised concerns about access to health care, activities and conditional release, including complaints that administrative and observation boards penalized women for non-participation caused by how prisons are run.
